What is sustainable packaging?
Okay, so you want to buy sustainable packaging for your jewellery brand . How do you find the right solution? First, we must find out what sustainability means. So, let us look at some of the ways in which a product can be eco-friendly/sustainable.
Sustainable certifications:

- FSC
Chemical properties:
- Water based

Other categories:

It is a rather extensive list, right? Well, it is just a small excerpt of all the different possible meanings the word sustainability has. And if you ever struggled to find a good definition of the word, this is the explanation.
“Sustainability means many different things all at once!
And it is as if the meanings have become entangled with each other.”
Did you know that there is a fancy word for words like that? Well, there is! Sustainability is a so-called polysemic word. And in such complicated cases, it can be a good idea to unravel the different meanings to avoid confusion. This is the only way to make sure we are talking about the same thing.
Therefore, we will unravel the concept of sustainability in the following. We do this by taking a closer look at all the categories listed above – one at a time. Simultaneously, we will highlight some of our jewellery packaging products that are relevant for each point.

Sustainable certifications
One of the first things consumers notice about packaging is the certifications. At Westpack, we have two main certifications: Fairtrade and FSC.
Fairtrade®
Fairtrade® is an international labelling system that focusses on better prices, decent working conditions, local sustainability, and fair terms of trade for farmers and workers in the developing world. By requiring companies to pay sustainable prices (which must never fall lower than the market price), Fairtrade addresses the injustices of conventional trade, which traditionally discriminates against the poorest, weakest producers. It enables them to improve their position and have control over their lives.
In 2018 we launched our first Fairtrade product at Westpack: Fairtrade jewellery pouches in cotton. The Fairtrade logo is visibly placed on these pouches, sewn in on the inside of the collar so that you can show your ethical commitment to your customers.

FSC®
FSC® stands for Forest Stewardship Council® and is an international nonprofit labelling system for wood and paper. The FSC mark guarantees that all materials come from an FSC forest, where no more wood is cut down than the forest can reproduce. This means better protection of the animaland plant life, and all foresters have proper training, safety equipment and a decent wage. There are three main reasons why Westpack is proud to be a certified member of FSC:
-
From an environmental perspective
In FSC forests, all harvesting activities are founded on the principle of sustained yields, which means that no more wood is cut down than the forest can regenerate. Furthermore, certified areas cannot be converted to other land use. And, just as important, the biodiversity of the areas is maintained or enhanced.
-
From a social perspective
FSC contributes to preserving and improving the social and economic well-being of local communities. They do this by supporting indigenous peoples’ rights of ownership. In other words, the local communities have fair access to resources and economic benefits.
-
From a governance perspective
The labour rights and employment conditions are improved. Much emphasis is placed on occupational health and safety.
Among our best-selling FSC products, you will find the jewellery boxes Boston ECO and Frankfurt ECO. These jewellery boxes have the FSC-brand printed underneath the base.

Related to the material’s life cycle
Some of our customers ask us: “what is the difference between recycled materials and recyclable materials?”. The simple answer is that recycled materials have previously existed in other forms – and recyclable materials have the potential to exist in new forms in the future.
To give an example: all glass bottles are recyclable because glass can be remelted effortlessly and turned into new objects. However, this does not mean that all glass bottles will be recycled (which the occasional scattered bottle at the roadside is a testament to). And it does not mean that all glass bottles are made from recycled glass.
It is the same with packaging. A cardboard jewellery box is considered to be recyclable when it is not treated with materials/chemicals that makes recycling impossible – but only your customer can make sure that the recycling actually happens.
But there is so much more to be said about this topic. In fact, it deserves an article of its own. Read more in our guide ‘Sustainability and Recycling’ .

Chemical properties
We would not make a sustainability guide without diving into the subject of chemical properties. In some ways, chemistry has gotten a bad name. As a natural phenomenon, it is an inescapable fact of life (there would be no life without it). As a field of study, it is crucial to other sciences (physics, biology, geology etc.). But in relation to consumer products, ‘chemistry’ has almost become synonymous with ‘toxic chemistry’.
Indeed, there has been numerous cases throughout history where toxic chemistry and consumer products were linked in unfortunate ways. But nowadays there are many regulations in place to protect consumers.
Still, there seems to be some confusion about which chemical properties are desirable in a product. Sometimes, you hear packaging producers talk about paper or cardboard being acid-free. Other times, you hear companies talk about how their packaging is compostable or biodegradable. Then there is the question of water-based glue and the term non-toxic. In this section, we will tell you exactly what these things imply.
Let us begin by clearing up what acid-free paper means.
Acid-free paper
You would be excused for thinking that paper “back in the good old days” was acid-free; perhaps produced locally by a nice old man in an idyllic papermill, until a big, polluting paper corporation came to town and put him out of business with their much-cheaper, much-whiter paper. Their secret? Evil acid, no doubt.
Well, this narrative is utterly wrong.
In fact, paper is not acid-free to begin with. Wood contains lignin which is quite acidic (it has a pH value of around 2). And because of this, paper that still contains lignin will turn yellow and deteriorate over time. That is partly what makes old paper look yellowish and antique. You know, like a pirate map.
A way to counteract this process is to treat the paper with a mild base so the acid is neutralized. Calcium is a great example of a mild base that can be used for this exact purpose. It has a pH value of 9,91 and is completely harmless to the environment. In fact, it is the most abundant mineral in the human body.
So, in an environmental perspective, there is not much of a difference between normal paper and acid-free paper. In most cases, they are equally eco-friendly. The same is true for normal tissue paper and acid-free tissue paper.
Why, then, do we offer both types of tissue paper in our online shop? Simply because there is a demand for both. Some of our customers prefer tissue paper with a neutral pH value. Perhaps they prefer it because some online packaging sellers claim that acid-free tissue paper will prevent tarnish in silver jewellery. That is an undocumented claim, and not at all our reason for having both types in our product range. We really want to highlight that point: no type of tissue paper is going to cause tarnish in jewellery. And we guarantee that all our products (including our standard tissue paper and our acid-free tissue paper) are 100% tarnish-free.

Compostable/biodegradable
Luckily, sustainability is something that most people care about these days. But only a couple of generations ago, it was frequently ignored. Say you had a jewellery shop in the 1950s. What selling points would you highlight to sell your jewellery?
Quality, yes. Design, of course.
But imagine how people would react if you began to explain the eco-friendly properties of a jewellery box to them. It would have been a tough crowd! Fortunately, all that has changed, and now composability is a huge selling point. In fact, it is more than probable that some of your customers have a compost pile in their backyard. And perhaps you even get the occasional question about whether your packaging is compostable?
We get that question a lot. And here is a detailed answer!
- All our gift boxes are compostable
- All our postal boxes are compostable
- All our paper carrier bags are compostable
- The following jewellery boxes are compostable:
Amsterdam, Barcelona ECO, Boston, Boston ECO, Frankfurt, Frankfurt ECO, Miami ECO, Santiago, Stockholm ECO
- Our cotton and linen jewellery pouches are compostable
But what exactly is the difference between the two terms ‘Compostable’ and ‘Biodegradable’? The short answer is that composting is a method that uses the natural process of biodegradation in a deliberate way.
Biodegradation is a naturally occurring process, where a material is broken down by microorganisms like bacteria and fungi. No human interaction is needed for the process to happen - it will happen anyways.
Composting occurs when humans “help biodegradation along” by piling waste that lends itself to fast biodegradation – and the process creates nutrient-rich soil without any traces of inorganic/toxic materials. This nutrient-rich soil can then be used to grow food or be used as a fertilizer!
Technically, nearly all materials are biodegradable. Even a plastic bag will biodegrade; it just takes more than 500 years. And it will take 1,000,000 years for a glass bottle to biodegrade, give or take a few thousand years.
Okay, now that we have described the difference between composting and biodegradation, let’s get back to how Westpack’s products can be composted.
The traditional foam inserts, found in many of our jewellery boxes, are not compostable and should be removed from the cardboard box before composting. With that out of the way, the remaining cardboard box is both recyclable and compostable.
Some of our customers occasionally ask us whether the foil we use for logo printing is compostable too. It is not. But it is worth keeping in mind that only a very low quantity of metal foil is used for each logo print. In fact, we did a calculation to find out. And it turns out that only 0.046 grammes of foil is used for each print! To us, that is an insignificant amount. Still, your customers are entitled to have a different opinion. And if you think they would object to even (extremely) low amounts of metal foil on their jewellery boxes, you may be better off ordering the boxes debossed without foil.

Water-based glue
We sometimes get questions about what type of glue we use in our jewellery boxes. And the short answer is that our glue is water-based. But what does that mean?
There are four types of water-based glues: vegetable glues, animal/protein glues, resin cements, and latex cements. At Westpack, we use protein glue. We will get back to that in a moment. But first, we will run through the different types of water-based glues to show you the differences between them.
- Vegetable glues are straightforward and cheap to make. Plant starches contain polymer chains of organic molecules, which can be used in adhesives. The starch can be found in both vegetables, plant roots, and seeds – and you probably already know how wheatpaste is used to make papier-mache. Vegetable glues are very compostable and eco-friendly .
- Animal/protein glue is a byproduct of meat production. The animal parts deemed inedible are used to create gelatin, which can be made into a strong and durable glue. It sounds harsh, but it is worth remembering that the animals are killed for meat anyways – the glue production simply uses the parts that would otherwise have gone to waste. Like vegetable glue, animal glue is very compostable . That is one of the reasons why Westpack uses protein glue.
- Resin cement is another type of water-based adhesive. The water acts as a carrier for a mixture of polymers (like PVA and EVA) – and when the cement dries, it becomes clear. This type of adhesive is great for bonding wood. Resin cements are neither compostable nor environmentally friendly .
- Latex cements are made of so-called “emulsified elastomers”. Loosely translated into everyday English, this means a blend of substances with elastic properties. This type of adhesive is almost always white. You probably know latex cements from self-stick envelopes and stamps. The adhesive can also be used to glue pieces of leather together (to make shoes etc.). Latex cements are generally not biodegradable .
If you are wondering why vegetable glues are not used more commonly, we can tell you that it has to do with functionality. Vegetable glue remains soluble in water, even after drying. And that poses a problem in most industries (including the jewellery and packaging industry). Imagine fixing a $1,000 diamond to a ring using a glue type that, almost instantaneously, dissolves in water. That would be a recipe for disaster. The same is true for jewellery boxes. We do not like the idea of your customers walking home in the rain, only to find the jewellery box partly dissolved.
In short: because of the inherent problems of using vegetable glues for jewellery boxes, we continue to use protein glue, which is very sturdy. You would be right to say that it is not the most eco-friendly solution, but it is the most eco-friendly viable solution. And by steering clear of resin cements and latex cements, we can make eco-friendly cardboard boxes that are both recyclable and compostable.

Non-toxic or skin friendly?
We sometimes scroll through Instagram to see what is going on in the world of jewellery. Occasionally, we come across posts that have the hashtag #nontoxic. And although that sounds great (and is technically true), it is a somewhat imprecise use of the word.
The definition of the word non-toxic is that the ingredients in a product will not cause injury or illness to a human when ingested, inhaled, or absorbed through the skin . That should be everyone’s minimum requirement for a product if you ask us.
So, what is going on here? We are pretty sure that non-toxic is sometimes used as a faulty synonym for skin friendly. In other words, jewelers who make their products without nickel will sometimes say that their jewellery is non-toxic.
Nickel has been used to make jewellery for centuries, and it is estimated that 2,5 million adults in the US suffer from nickel allergy. It is something that should be taken very seriously. But although nickel certainly will cause illness to an allergic person when absorbed through the skin (as in the definition), it is still not toxic, legally speaking, as long as the release of nickel stays below a certain permitted threshold level – which can vary from one country to another. For that reason, we think it would be more precise to use the word skin friendly or similar. And there is another reason.
In other industries, mainly cosmetics, the term non-toxic has become a huge marketing buzzword; sometimes strategically used to make it seem like competing products contain toxic materials. Luckily, most countries have legislations that prevent toxic products from ever reaching the shelves of the local store. So, in many cases, the non-toxic label is abundant information - something that goes without saying, like: “this ice cream is completely meat-free”. That is our opinion on the subject, anyways.
But what about Westpack’s products? Are they skin friendly? Yes, they are. The only exception is our professional jewellery cleaning products, which are not meant to be skin friendly in the first place (you should always wear gloves when you use these products).

Related to animal welfare
Many of our customers ask us questions related to animal welfare, so we thought that we would include a section about it in our sustainability guide. A question we get from time to time is: “are your products vegan?” And the short answer is: sometimes.
The only aim of this content is to educate for the purpose of education. Whether or not humans have the right to use animals for food, clothes, products, testing etc. is a discussion we will not go into here. Our fundamental standpoint is that animals should always be treated humanely.
The reason why some of our products are not vegan is that several of our cardboard production machines use glue that contains gelatin, which is an animal-derived byproduct. In fact, this type of glue is the industry standard for these types of boxes.
If you are a vegan/vegetarian, this may seem unappealing to you. But as we have mentioned earlier in the article, it is worth remembering that the animals are killed for meat anyways. The glue is simply made from byproducts (the parts that would otherwise have gone to waste).
Some people think that protein glue is made from horses. That is very rarely the case. Historically, horses have been sent to glue factories when they died, because they contain high levels of collagen, which can be turned into gelatin.
Nowadays, though, it would not make much sense for a glue factory to wait around for horses to die. The steady flow of byproducts from meat production is much more predictable and cost-efficient.
There is also another misconception we want to clear up. Many people think that vegan and cruelty free is the same thing, but there are important differences:
- When something is vegan , it means that the product is not made from animal-derived ingredients. In other words, the term only refers to the production process.
- When something is cruelty free , it means that the product has not been tested on animals. In other words, the term does not refer to the production process - only the subsequent testing.
It is also worth mentioning that there is no legislation regarding the use of the term cruelty free . So, in theory, any company can use it.

Production practices
There are many different production paradigms, but we will focus on Organic and Zero-waste.
Organic
Some words are easy to comprehend, yet difficult to define. Organic is one of those words. As soon as you hear it, you instinctively feel that the product in question is more natural and (maybe) healthier than other kinds of products. But if someone asked you for a definition, what would it be?
We will give you a minute to think…
One way to explain the term is to draw attention to the fact that all farming was organic until the middle of the 19th century (that was when artificial fertilizers were introduced).
Basically, anything synthetic/modified/artificial is a no-go in organic farming. This means that synthetic pesticides are prohibited, as are genetically modified seeds.
Furthermore, organic farming often uses methods like crop rotation, composting, and biological pest control. Pesticides are allowed in organic farming; the only requirement is that the substance can also be found in nature.
To be certified organic, a product must live up to certain standards set by the country in which it is sold. This means that a product that is certified organic in the US is not necessarily certified organic in France.

Zero-waste
Imagine if you were not allowed to produce any waste in your everyday life. No plastic bags. No cereal boxes. No food packaging. That would be impossible, right?
Well, nonetheless that is the principle of zero-waste. In other words, the goal is that no trash is dumped in landfills or sent to incineration .
In the last 10-15 years, the zero-waste movement has become very popular. And still more people try to live up to the ideal. Many bloggers and influencers share their tricks for generating less waste in their everyday life. Some of these tricks include:
- Refuse to accept information flyers or business cards
- Bring sacks to the wholefoods store (for beans, nuts, fruit etc.)
- Practice composting
Some business owners even try to incorporate the principle in their value chain. But as production practices go, zero-waste is a very tough one to achieve. All materials and tools you purchase will generate waste simply because of the associated packaging.
As a packaging supplier, we focus on developing packaging that can be recycled or composted. In fact, most of our jewellery boxes and postal boxes are recyclable.
Some of our customers occasionally ask us whether the foil we use for logo printing is compostable. It is not. But it is worth keeping in mind that an average of only 0.046 grammes of foil is used for each logo print (as we mentioned earlier in the guide).
Still, some customers choose to have the jewellery boxes debossed without foil. As you can see in the picture, the logo still looks good. This could be a good option if you want your jewellery boxes to be completely compostable. But keep in mind that the logo will draw less attention to itself.
In some cases (if you are ordering many items), we can also print your logo using soy-ink! Contact our sales department if you think this option could be right for you.
As the name indicates, soy-ink is based on refined soybean oil. And although it is not edible, it is much more eco-friendly than its petroleum-based counterpart. Soy-ink can be removed more easily than regular ink during a recycling process, which is a big plus. It is also more sustainable to produce, simply because soybeans are a renewable resource.

Carbon neutral
Still more companies talk about how their company has become carbon neutral. But what does that really mean? Let us say that you (completely hypothetically) decided to run a carbon neutral jewellery business.
Should small businesses account/compensate for their greenhouse gas emissions? As a company, we do not have an opinion on the matter. We are not moralizing or recommending that any actions should be taken – neither are we trying to steer the climate debate in a certain direction. Our only aim with this content is to educate for the purpose of education.
Still more companies talk about how their company has become carbon neutral. But what does that really mean? Let us say that you (completely hypothetically) decided to run a carbon neutral jewellery business. That would require you to:
- Calculate (or at least come up with a qualified estimate) of your business’ combined emissions.
- Find ways to optimize your value chain to reduce emissions.
- Buy carbon credits to compensate for the remaining emissions.
If you own a small jewellery business, no one will expect you to do any of the above – and no one has the right to expect it. But hypothetically, if you think it could be relevant, this is one way to approach it:
The calculation
It can be very complicated to calculate emissions. You would have to include things like your electricity, water use, and heat consumption (If you have a workshop,) as well as the shipping to your customers etc. There are many guides on the internet with facts about the different calculations. Remember that you do not have to make a perfect calculation – if you can come up with a good estimate based on the data you have access to, at least you are doing something.
The optimization
This is where you take a critical look at your value chain and look for things that can be optimized. Could you change some of the lighting in your workshop? There are many ways to make small adjustments. Be as creative as you can.
The compensation
No matter how many adjustments you make, your business will still leave a carbon footprint. But if you have a good estimate of your emissions, you know how many carbon credits it would take to compensate. Have you heard of carbon credits? The fundamental principle is simple. If you leave a carbon footprint, you could pay someone to wipe out an equally sized footprint somewhere else. There are three basic types of carbon credits:
- Credits from reduced emissions: If a company has reduced their emissions significantly, they can sell the reduced amount as carbon credits.
- Credits from removed emissions: Carbon capture projects removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere – as do trees. In other words, new green technology and planting of forests could be the way forward.
- Credits from avoided emissions: If someone has the legal right to a piece of forest, but refrains from cutting it down, they can sell the avoided emissions as carbon credits.

ECO-friendly shipping of jewellery
You often hear that airplane travel is bad for the environment. Does this mean that shipping jewellery by plane is not eco-friendly? And how does airfreight compare to sea, road, and rail transportation? We have made an illustrative example to answer these questions.
Imagine a jewellery designer living in London. She specializes in bracelets, which she ships all over the world. A customer in Rome just placed an order for a limited-edition bracelet that comes in a wooden jewellery box. The bracelet, the jewellery box, and the postal box (including a small catalog) weighs a combined 1,2 kg.
How would different means of transportation influence the amount of greenhouse gas emissions associated with the shipping? Let us look at the numbers:
- Train: By sending the bracelet to the customer by train, a mere 0.022 kg of CO 2 e is released to the atmosphere. However, packages are rarely shipped by train.
- Sea ship: This type of freight is typically the most sustainable option. However, the sea route from London to Rome is rather long, which means that this type of transportation is only the second-best option in this case. 0.073 kg of CO 2 e is released to the atmosphere.
- Truck: When packages are sent to addresses on the same continent, they are very often sent by road haul. If the order for the Italian customer is delivered by truck/road haul, 0.16 kg of CO 2 e is released to the atmosphere.
- Airplane: Fighting gravity takes a lot of energy. Consequently, the greenhouse gas emissions for airfreight are often 75 times larger than those from rail freight. If the British jewellery designer sends the jewellery by plane, a staggering 1.66 kg of CO 2 e is released to the atmosphere.
What does this mean for you as a jewellery designer?
We are not saying that you should never ship your jewellery (or other products) by airfreight. If your customers expect to receive the jewellery fast, the matter is out of your hands. If you insist on having a longer delivery time (because you do not want to ship your products by plane), you risk losing the customer. So, what can you do? Well, you can inform the customer about the difference in greenhouse gas emissions and let them decide which option they prefer.
As a general rule, shipping by sea and rail is more sustainable than shipping by road – and airfreight is in a category of its own.

In summation
As you can see, products can be sustainable in many different ways. In some cases, the eco-friendly aspect is related to the material’s life-cycle, and in other cases, it is related to its chemical properties. Sometimes it is connected to the way the product was produced, manufactured, or tested, but it can also concern animal welfare and CO 2 emissions.
The important question for you to ask yourself is what areas of sustainability that you find most essential.

Beware of greenwashing
Our guess is that you are a critical consumer – based on the fact that you are reading this article. And one thing that consumers should always be critical of is greenwashing: the pitfall to avoid for any company. Greenwashing is reminiscent of political spin. It is a communication strategy with the aim of manipulating the recipient/voter/consumer into thinking that something is more eco-friendly than it actually is.
To make an analogy, you could look at the stereotypical real estate agent that is often portrayed in comedies. The real estate agent typically says some flattering things about the house he is trying to sell but holds back critical information at the same time. Like this:
- There is great ventilation throughout the house (because of all the holes in the roof).
- The house has been continuously renovated (it had to, because of the recurring floods)
- It is a friendly neighborhood (which means that everyone will help you pack, once you discover the severe termite infestation)
Greenwashing works the same way. But instead of a deceitful realtor, you will meet a deceptive product description. The packaging of various products may read:
- With 100% more organic ingredients (before it was 0.01%, now it is 0.02%)
- Completely vegan and calorie-free (because it is a bottle of water – what else would you expect?)
- Made from 100% recycled plastic (the label is placed on the lid. Conveniently, the statement refers only to the lid)
- Make your life greener (although you are using these chemical rich laundry detergents)
- With nature’s colours (in other words: not natural colours, but nuances you can find in the natural world, if you look hard for them)
Basically, greenwashing is what happens when a company tries to capitalize on customer demands by conveying a false impression of being green. We are sure you know what to look for.
