Use the lessons below as hypotheses and working methods, and say which talk an idea comes from when you use it.

## When to read this

Before writing or judging ad creative, ad or email copy, briefing the content team, choosing formats, planning a
measurement or budget question, or building agents in biblen. The "Standing lessons" section is the summary; open
`speaker-notes.md` for the talk itself.

## Standing lessons for Westpack

### Creative and content

1. **Brief one situation, not a target group.** A category entry point (CEP) is a concrete situation that sends
   someone hunting in the category. One CEP per video, situation first, Westpack visible early, the product as the
   answer. NORMAL's example: not "24 products for an advent calendar" but a parent who, late in November, is short of
   presents for the children's calendar. (Cecilie Kjærgaard, NORMAL.) Candidate Westpack CEPs, to validate with the
   team: opening a shop or a new collection; the Christmas rush and gift wrapping; webshop orders that must look
   good on unboxing; a new logo or rebrand; a fair, market or pop-up; running low on boxes before a peak.
2. **Brand early, without shouting.** Situation content with no clear sender only grows the category. NORMAL puts a
   NORMAL bag with logo and colours in the background of a home scene. Westpack has no written list of distinctive
   assets (colour, typeface, uniform, recurring devices); the rule needs one.
3. **New concepts, not near-identical versions.** With Andromeda and personalisation, a new person, angle, offer or
   format is a new concept; a different headline or button colour is not. Take one of the best existing messages and
   move it into a new format. (Halfdan Timm, Obsidian.) This matches how we judge ads: pooled by concept, not as
   single variants.
4. **Cheap, native-looking statics are a real format.** Phone shots, real people, little text, or text as the whole
   idea: a meme, a Snapchat-style text box on a photo, a wall of text, a handwritten yellow note placed by the
   product, deliberately simple Paint-style humour. Video is not required, and short videos run 15-20 s.
   (Halfdan Timm.) Godt Smil swapped only the visual of a webinar ad, same copy: 529 kr per lead polished, 86 kr
   skewed. (Claus Rump.) cBrain: people ads 10.86 % CTR against 0.87 % for brand ads. (Helene Vest-Hansen.) These are
   anecdotes. At Westpack the format verdict flips with the attribution reading, so never call a format better on
   one reading (`ad-level-evidence-limits`).
5. **Test a person as the sender.** Same creative, audience, timing and link, but posted by a relevant person's page
   rather than the company page, gave better attention in Halfdan's test. Not tried at Westpack. It fits the B2B
   rule that people buy from people, and employees on camera are welcome (`placeholder-campaign-and-content-library`).
6. **Every creative wears out: same wine, new bottles.** When the product and offers are fixed, change the angle,
   the wording and the pictures. Plan three skewed ads and put the follow-up in the calendar. (Claus Rump, Godt Smil.)
   For how long Westpack's ads actually last, see the creative-lifetime analysis (2026-11-06) in memory.
7. **Local relevance does not come from translation.** NORMAL buys local creators for capacity, language and
   culture. UGC means NORMAL is the sender (it buys production and variants). A partnership ad makes the creator a
   co-sender (it buys credibility and their audience). Micro-creators bring niche relevance. Flying Tiger lets AI
   translate HQ copy but the local market approves it, because humour and jargon do not survive. For Westpack this is
   a hypothesis against our translation-heavy practice (Duplicate & translate, the Trustpilot template, DE/FR/IT
   voice-overs), not a rule. What our data shows: the JOYO customer video won with **Westpack as the sender**
   (about 920 kr per new customer) and was weak as a partnership ad. For a B2B brand the "creators" are existing
   customers, with explicit rights (Muschelgold gave none).
8. **Video craft.**
   - Spend the first 1-3 seconds on the viewer's problem, with name and background later, and keep one message per
     video. (Olivia Lindegaard Pedersen.)
   - Change the picture every 2-3 seconds. Film vertically, with the camera at eye level and the eyes in the top
     third. (Olivia Lindegaard Pedersen.)
   - Make the hook state the promise within 2-5 seconds, and end tight: credits and logo outros make people leave.
     Use the retention graph to find where viewers drop. (Fredrik Plejdrup.)
   - Simone (Flying Tiger) gives a hook in the first three seconds, little text and a clear gain or loss.
9. **AI video from what we already have.** One good product photo can become many situations and seasons. Animate
   an existing ad image (Kling). Keep templates with fixed and variable parts. Change one thing at a time. Check the
   product, motion and sound against the original: a wireless lamp got a cable, and a lamp got a click it does not
   have. (Nikolaj Lykke Viborg.) Højkant mixes real footage and AI per part of the video. Their advice is to record
   the voice yourself and then change its character, rather than use pure text-to-speech, and to label realistic
   manipulation. Halfdan warns that artificial-looking AI people draw negative reactions, which agrees with the
   standing exclusion of "AI billeder".
10. **Design for sharing, and answer inside the post.** Ask whether someone would send this to a named friend. Put
    the question and the useful answer in the asset itself, without "link in bio". Pick the format by how the
    information is used: a still for one fact, a carousel to break a complex topic into parts, a video for an
    explanation or a person. (elbdudler.)
11. **Content sources have different jobs.** Flying Tiger: HQ content builds the shared brand story, local content
    catches attention, influencer content holds it, and creators supply volume for products the team cannot cover.
    HQ sends a base package of four posts a week. (Simone Brændgaard Tolstrup.)

### Copy

- **"Og hvad så?"** Write what you want to say, then ask "so what?" again and again until you reach a gain or a
  consequence the reader cares about. Start the text there. (Line Ingwar Karlsen.)
- **The comeback cheat code ("comeback-snydekoden")** is a two-column table. On the left go the audience's known and
  likely objections; on the right, a concrete answer to each. Every answer can become its own ad angle. "This isn't
  for a business like mine" is answered with a customer that looks like them. In the W47 Kevin email each route is
  headed by a sentence the shop owner says, and the answer under it is the comeback.
- **The pancake test ("pandekagetesten")**: write the opposite of the claim. If nobody would ever say the opposite,
  the claim says nothing. Replace it with a concrete promise the customer can hold you to: not "Vi prioriterer god
  kundeservice" but "Vi svarer inden for en time", if it is true.
- **Four language rules**: "du" and "vi", not "man"; cut filler; active verbs; human language with air in it. Make
  the language vivid with small images such as "hamre i tastaturet", and dare to give the audience something to
  react to. "Don't be a blå Klovborg", the most ordinary sliced cheese: do not be the interchangeable option. (Line
  Ingwar Karlsen; the user's notes.)
- **USPs must be a real reason to choose us.** Hold our ads up against competitors' and ask why a customer would
  react to ours. Make each USP tangible: a number, a picture, a proof. (Claus Rump.) The user's note: four USPs carry
  Meta. Take them only from `knowledge/westpack-site/README.md`. Samples are not free, and logo print is included
  only on jewellery boxes.
- These rules apply to emails too (`copywriter-guide-applies-to-emails`). "du not man", passive voice and the
  pancake test can be checked mechanically. They are candidates for the Email check and the subject-line rules, not
  yet built.

### Measurement and data

- **The measurement ladder** comes from Jacob Holst Mouritzen (spelled with z; earlier notes here had "Mouritsen").
  The steps are response; reported return; brand signals such as share of search, direct and brand traffic,
  mentions and qualified enquiries; brand measurement; MMM; tests (geo); attribution; brand-equity modelling; and
  forecasting. A higher reported ROAS can sit beside falling revenue when spend concentrates on people who would
  have bought anyway.
- **Qualified awareness** is the steering metric: knowing the name is not knowing what we sell or when it is
  relevant. In his furniture case 70 % knew the name but many knew only the name. The MMM case: 90m revenue, 72m
  modelled baseline, 18m marketing. MMM needs variation in spend; tests supply it. Westpack's research on all of
  this, with our own numbers, is `knowledge/meta-research/07-measurement-ladder.md`.
- **Follow the lead or customer to real money, in your own data.** Godt Smil's case:
  - Meta reported 62 leads from a landing page, and Godt Smil's own system had 22.
  - A booked patient cost 1,629 kr via the landing page and 783 kr via lead forms.
  - They separate cost per lead from cost per booked patient.

  Lead forms suit lead generation, and webshops are a different case. (Claus Rump.) Gavmild joins ad-platform data
  with sales and anonymised customer data, judges leads on quality, and documents decisions and reasons so others
  can take over. (Christian Kyed Vestergaard.) This is what the Navision cohort work does for Westpack.
- **Engagement is not automatically good.** Flying Tiger's product-recall posts were among the most engaging.
  Read engagement with sentiment and judge content against its purpose.
- **Consent decides the data.** A consent flow must be voluntary, informed, granular, explicit, in advance, easy to
  withdraw and documented. Missing consent signals to Google and Meta weaken their data. (Cookiebot.) See
  `meta-tracking-is-consent-gated`.
- **TV in three layers** (Gavmild): direct response around the spot, the derived effect on other channels, and the
  whole mix in statistical models. Small reallocations matter a lot, so models need critical review.

### B2B

- **Reach the whole buying group, and rate Meta higher in B2B.** Exposure is cheaper there than on LinkedIn, and
  the content itself finds the audience through who stops, stays and clicks. (Morten Bie.) cBrain found Meta "yderst
  effektiv" even selling to German ministries. Louise Philipsen (LinkedIn) also recommends testing Meta on price per
  relevant contact. This supports `westpack-meta-audience-is-b2b`.
- **Talk about the customer's reality**, not "innovative, holistic solutions": "the forklift that stands still". Take
  Q&A from sales and customer service, because their questions are the objections that block a purchase, and they
  feed the comeback table. Not every ad needs to end in a form. (Morten Bie.)
- **Make the next step easy for the ready**: an offer on the thank-you page, repeated in a mail, and fast follow-up.
  (Morten Bie.)
- **Personas from inside**: cBrain interviewed four colleagues with 25-40 years on the customer side instead of
  buying reports. The Westpack equivalent is customer service and sales on why customers stay or leave, which the
  customer-lifetime analysis recommends anyway.
- **Stay present**: purchases happen when need and timing meet, so keep in touch through videos, webinars and mail.
  (Morten Bie.) Visit Aarhus builds its own next steps, guides and games leading to sign-ups and mail flows, and
  keeps contact through a decision period (Johan Friis Bergholt). The Westpack equivalents are the Boxulator blog
  game and Signup Lab.

### Working with AI and agents

- **Meta's loop** (Carolina Frenning): build a brand agent first, then audience and platform agents. Score concepts
  before a person sees them. A person approves, and **the human always publishes**. Specific corrections become
  standing rules (her example: "product hero always"), and lessons carry into the next campaign. This is how this
  repo already works (CLAUDE.md, memory, `never-switch-ads-on`). Her caution: expect the first run to need
  teaching, and the gain is where time goes, not large time savings.
- **Your own tools for your own taste.** Olivia built a video editor with Claude Code: FFmpeg cuts, Whisper writes
  time-coded transcripts, Playwright animates graphics and Apple Vision frames the eyes. Each brand has a board of
  tone, logo, colours and reference videos. Feedback becomes saved editing rules, for example "cut pauses over half
  a second" or "subtitles follow the spoken words". She approves takes and a storyboard before the final cut.
  NORMAL's creator web app handles sign-up, product data from the PIM, upload, corrections, a register of usage
  rights and generated ad copy. A rights register would have made the Muschelgold case checkable. Not built.
- **Changing habits** (Anders Colding-Jørgensen): choose one concrete change, budget the dip in productivity while
  learning, and repeat the new action 5-10 times. More knowledge alone does not change a routine.
